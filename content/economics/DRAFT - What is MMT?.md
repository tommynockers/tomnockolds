Modern Monetary Theory (MMT) is a heterodox school of economic thought that places primary importance on understanding the institutions and systems that underpin the monetary systems in nation state economies. As a school of thought whose principal ideas and arguments have been developed in the academic arena, MMT provides a 'lens' or 'perspective' for analysis of macroeconomics. MMT is also associated with policy proposals. When considering these, it it important to start by recognising that the analysis comes first, and policy proposals emerge later from the insights provided by the analytical lens.

# Heterodox vs Orthodox
Before MMT can be properly described it is important to provide some basic context regarding the environment from which MMT has emerged over the last 25 plus years.

Economics is unlike other fields of academic inquiry in that it is dominated by a single, or narrow, school of economic thought - Neoclassical Economics. [Almost all economists are neoclassical economists](what-is-heterodox-economics?) who vigorously oppose other schools of thought. Orthodox (Neoclassical) economics [suffers heavily from group-think](https://billmitchell.org/blog/?p=60864). It shuns those who oppose the dominant ideas and prevents its central tenets from being directly or meaningfully challenged.

Despite this, there are academic economists who are outside the neoclassical (orthodox) school. Collectively they are known as heterodox economists. The schools in heterodox economics include: Marxism, Post Keynesianism and Institutionalism, and Ecological Economics, among others. The fact that there is (in effect) a single school within Orthodox Economics but multiple schools in Heterodox Economics should raise questions about why this is the case. (the answer is group- think).

The dominance of neoclassical economics extends beyond the universities to the economists working in government, on government, or in the private sector. In this realm it is even less common to find persons working as economists who are not trained in the neoclassical school.

Neoclassical economics when applied in the political realm is what is known as neoliberalism. More correctly, neoliberalism is a political project that emerged from neoclassical economics.

# MMT builds on earlier ideas

MMT is a heterodox school of economic thinking. Specifically, it builds on ideas primarily drawn from Institutionalism and Post Keynesianism.

Synthesising ideas that already exist in the world - merging them with other ideas and redescrbiing them in a way that is relevant to the world as it is currently known and understood - is a completely normal practice in academia, or general thinking for that matter, and everything in between.

# A new idea at the heart of MMT

However, despite this fact that MMT is a synthesis of existing ideas, it is also true that it incorporates a new idea that previously did not exist in the economic academy. MMT reverses the sequence of government spending, taxation and borrowing. Prior to MMT, all economists subscribed to the idea that governments must tax or borrow before they can spend (TAB-S) However what MMT says is that governments, as the sole source of the units they demand for taxation (i.e. government money), must spend the money into existence before the money is available in the economy and therefore is available for the private sector to use as payment of taxes and purchase of government debt (S-TAB).

This new idea is central to the 'lens' for analysis that MMT provides. It comes from a revival of Chartalism, which gained some prominence in the early 20th century and can be traced back to classical economists such as Adam Smith. But while Chartalism sees money as a creation of the state, it does not reverse the order of operations. And the reason why Chartalism didn't do this is simple - before the modern period (since the second half of the 20th Century) all governments had to carefully manage their money supplies and gold and silver reserves.

But since the abolition of the gold standard in the 1970s, most economies are pure fiat economies, meaning there is no external commodity or money-like thing that backs the currency. There is nothing that the government must agree to convert the currency to on demand. Without any obligation to conversion to gold, or anything else, there is no need to collect anything before government spending takes place.

Interestingly, MMT got its name from one of its two founders - Bill Mitchell - but before that name came along, it was often referred to as Neo-Chartalism, especially by the other co-founder, Warren Mosler.

# Taxes in the modern economy

While MMT says that governments don't need to tax or borrow before they spend, it does address some key concerns that arise when people think about this new idea.

According to MMT, the first purpose of taxation is to create demand in the economy for the currency in the first place. This works because each citizen in a country is required to pay taxes. Failure to pay taxes is a serious offence, resulting in sanctions such as fines or even a criminal conviction and a custodial sentence.

By demanding payment of taxes in the unit of account that only the government itself can create, the government makes it a vitally important thing for everyone to find a job or some other means of paying those taxes. The imposition of taxes means everyone is looking to collect the government-tax-tokens that only the government can create.

MMT also says that through taxes and spending, the government is the source of employment in the economy. You are (involuntarily) unemployed if you are looking for work to do in exchange for government-tax-tokens but cannot find such work. One way of thinking about this is to say that the government created the money in the first place, at the same time as creating the tax liability... but if there is not enough tokens in the economy then there must also be people who cannot find enough work to collect enough government-tax-tokens. The government is responsible for taxes, but it is also responsible for ensuring there is enough government-tax-tokens in the economy to enable everyone to pay their tax bill.

It also helps if you think of taxes as the means by which the government provisions itself. Once the government has announced the tax liability it means there are people looking for work. The government can then hire those people to do the work the government wants them to do for the government. In return, it will pay those people a certain amount of government-tax-tokens.

# The Government is a monopoly price-setter

The government is the only entity in the economy that can create government-tax-tokens. Yes, it is true that banks operate under a government licence to create new bank-money by issuing loans, but the bank-money created this way is not the same thing as government-tax-tokens. Too complicated to explain in detail, but it is enough to say that there are different levels of money in the economy. Government-tax-tokens are the highest-powered level of money-ness in the economy. Bank-money, while still being very powerful, is not the same thing and is not as powerful as government-tax-tokens. 

Because the government has a monopoly over its tax-tokens, this means it the sole price-setter of its tokens. Whatever the government says you need to do to earn the tax-tokens - such as working 40 hours a week to earn 1,000 tokens - that then sets the price level in all other parts of the economy.

ASIDE: MMT founder Warren Mosler points out that since monopoly is a microeconomic concept, this monopoly-price-setter idea in MMT is the microeconomic foundation upon which MMT rests. This is an academic thing - economists like to claim their theories rest on microeconomic foundations and they like to claim opposing theories/schools don't have micro-foundations.

Neoclassical economics has no theory of the price level. It can point to inflation and deflation as changing the price level, but it cannot explain where the original price level came from.

# Policy implications

Before talking about policy, a reminder that MMT is first and foremost an analytical 'perspective' that provides a 'lens for analysis' for the modern nation-state economy.

However, this analysis - particularly the revelations above - do have some immediate and pressing policy implications. For this reason, MMT is often incorrectly thought of as being an inherently political project, or is thought of as being a thing that you 'do' - such as a government might "Implement MMT".

MMT does not say anybody should 'do' anything - other than it says people should take its analytical frame seriously. But when you do take the analysis seriously, you are immediately presented with policy considerations.

Questions emerge such as
- "If the government is not constrained in its spending by needing to first tax or borrow, then why do governments run their budgets as if they were a household or a business?"
- "If the government is the source of unemployment in the economy, then can we do something about that?"
- "if governments can spend without restriction, then what prevents them from pushing too much money into the economy, causing excessive demand and inflation?"
- "If the government doesn't need to borrow, then what is the purpose of issuing government debt, and should we keep doing this?"

Despite this, however, there is one policy prescription that sits at the heart of MMT. So yes it is wrong to think of MMT as being political and asking people to 'do' anything - however the contradiction to this is the idea of a Job Guarantee. Developed initially by co-founder Bill Mitchell, a Job Guarantee is a policy mechanism that would need to be enacted so as to provide a better (more effective, more efficient, more ethical) method for managing inflation in the economy.
